UV System Annual Service: The Lamp, the Sleeve and What Actually Keeps It Working

Learn what residential UV service should include: model-specific lamp replacement, quartz-sleeve cleaning, pretreatment, alarms and water-quality checks.

Published November 21, 2026 · Brian Paul, Pure Water Pros

A UV lamp can still glow even when the system is overdue for service. Visible light is not proof that a residential UV system is delivering its intended disinfection performance.

Reliable operation depends on the correct lamp or lamp unit, a clean quartz sleeve, suitable incoming water, controlled flow, working alarms and continued private-well testing. “Annual UV service” is useful shorthand for many conventional systems, but the exact interval and procedure must follow the model.

Start with the model and manual

Before ordering a lamp or opening the chamber, record:

  • manufacturer and model;
  • controller model and display;
  • lamp or lamp-unit part number;
  • installation date and last service date;
  • alarm history;
  • prefilter type;
  • plumbing and bypass position; and
  • whether an automatic shutoff or flow-control device is installed.

Many conventional residential UV lamps have a useful life of approximately 9,000 hours and are replaced annually in a full-time residence. Other systems use different lamp units or replacement intervals. The controller and owner's manual—not a generic anniversary date—govern the exact requirement.

Use the replacement specified for the installed model. Similar-looking lamps are not automatically interchangeable.

What the UV lamp does

The lamp produces ultraviolet energy inside a quartz sleeve. Water passes through the chamber around that sleeve. Under the system's specified flow and water conditions, the UV dose can inactivate microorganisms by preventing them from reproducing.

UV output changes as a lamp ages. That is why a lamp may look bright to the eye after its effective service interval has passed. Do not reset a lamp-life reminder without replacing the lamp or completing the procedure required by the manufacturer.

Why the quartz sleeve matters

The lamp is separated from the water by a quartz sleeve. Iron, manganese, hardness, sediment or other deposits can coat the sleeve and reduce the amount of UV energy reaching the water.

Service may include:

  1. shutting down and isolating the system according to the manual;
  2. allowing the chamber to cool;
  3. draining or relieving pressure;
  4. removing the lamp and sleeve without touching the lamp glass;
  5. inspecting the sleeve for film, scale, clouding, cracks or chips;
  6. cleaning or replacing the sleeve as required;
  7. inspecting and replacing seals according to the manufacturer's procedure;
  8. reassembling, leak-testing and restarting; and
  9. confirming normal controller and alarm status.

UV light can injure eyes and skin, and a chamber can contain hot water after a period of no flow. Follow the electrical, pressure and cooling steps in the manual.

Pretreatment protects UV performance

UV systems require water that transmits ultraviolet light effectively. Particles can scatter or shield light, while iron, manganese, hardness and colour can foul the sleeve or absorb UV.

Current VIQUA guidance for many residential systems lists inlet conditions around:

  • turbidity below 1 NTU;
  • iron below 0.3 mg/L;
  • manganese below 0.05 mg/L;
  • hardness below 7 grains per gallon or 120 mg/L as calcium carbonate; and
  • UV transmittance above 75 percent.

Those figures are not universal design limits for every manufacturer or model. Check the installed system's manual and certification. The important correction is that turbidity suitable for UV is not “roughly 20 NTU”; it is commonly required to be far lower.

Sediment prefiltration is often installed ahead of a residential UV chamber. Additional treatment may be required when iron, manganese, hardness, colour or other conditions exceed the UV system's inlet limits.

Service the installed prefilters

A clogged cartridge can reduce household pressure and flow. A bypassed, missing or unsuitable filter can allow sediment to reach the sleeve.

During service:

  • identify each cartridge by size and micron rating;
  • check its condition and replacement history;
  • inspect housings and seals;
  • verify that the cartridge is appropriate for the water and flow;
  • replace carbon only where carbon is actually part of the installed treatment goal; and
  • sanitize and reassemble housings according to the equipment instructions.

A “change every filter annually” rule is too broad. Cartridge life depends on water quality, use, pressure loss and manufacturer guidance.

Check flow and treatment order

A UV unit must be sized for the maximum flow that can pass through it. If flow exceeds the system's validated or rated capacity, contact time and UV dose can fall below the intended performance.

Confirm:

  • model flow rating;
  • any installed flow restrictor or regulator;
  • peak household demand;
  • chamber orientation and service access;
  • whether the bypass is fully closed during normal use; and
  • that iron, sediment or other required pretreatment occurs before UV.

UV is usually placed near the end of the treatment sequence so particles and fouling contaminants are addressed first.

Verify the controller and alarms

After service, confirm the controller shows normal operation and test the alarm functions using the manufacturer's procedure. Depending on the model, check:

  • lamp-life timer;
  • audible and visual alarms;
  • UV sensor condition;
  • automatic shutoff or solenoid operation, if equipped;
  • remote alarm contacts, if equipped; and
  • leak-free operation.

Do not silence a persistent alarm and treat that as a repair. Identify why the alarm occurred.

Restart correctly

Some VIQUA instructions say not to pass water through the UV system for at least five minutes after power is applied, including after a power interruption. Other restart steps can include flushing hot water that accumulated in the chamber during a no-flow period.

Follow the exact manual. If untreated water passed through the system or bypass during service, downstream plumbing may require a specific disinfection and flushing procedure before normal use.

UV service does not replace water testing

HealthLink BC recommends bacteriological testing of private wells at least annually and chemical testing on an ongoing annual basis. A normal controller display cannot prove that a well-water sample is free of E. coli or total coliforms.

Retest after events that may affect water quality, including flooding, well work, a suspected contamination event or a treatment interruption where untreated water may have entered the plumbing.

A practical UV service checklist

  • Identify the exact system and manual.
  • Review alarms and service history.
  • Isolate power and pressure safely.
  • Replace the lamp or lamp unit when due for that model.
  • Inspect and clean or replace the quartz sleeve.
  • Replace model-specified seals as required.
  • Inspect installed prefilters and housings.
  • Check water quality against the UV inlet limits.
  • Confirm peak flow and treatment order.
  • Restore power and observe the required no-flow warm-up.
  • Leak-test and verify controller and alarm operation.
  • Complete any required downstream disinfection or flushing.
  • Record the service date, parts and next due interval.
  • Continue qualified-laboratory well-water testing.

Frequently asked questions

Does every UV lamp need replacement once a year?

No. Many conventional residential lamps are rated around 9,000 hours and are replaced annually, but models differ. Follow the controller and manual for the installed system.

Can I clean the lamp instead of replacing it?

The quartz sleeve is the component commonly cleaned. A lamp or lamp unit is replaced at its specified service interval.

Why does the sleeve keep fouling?

Test for hardness, iron, manganese, turbidity, colour and other conditions that can deposit on the sleeve or interfere with UV. Pretreatment may need service or correction.

Is a green light proof that the water is safe?

No. The display is one operational check. Flow, water quality, sleeve condition, lamp age, installation and laboratory testing still matter.

Does Greater Victoria municipal water need household UV?

Usually not. CRD municipal water is already treated, disinfected and monitored. Household UV should follow a defined need, not be added automatically.
