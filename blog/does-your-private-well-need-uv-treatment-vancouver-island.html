Does Your Private Well Need UV Treatment? Bacteria Testing and Disinfection on Vancouver Island

A practical guide to bacteria testing, pretreatment, UV sizing and maintenance for private-well properties on Vancouver Island.

Published September 16, 2026 · Brian Paul, Pure Water Pros

If you own a private well on Vancouver Island, you may be considering UV treatment because of a positive bacteria result, recurring water-quality concerns or an older UV system that needs service. UV can be a useful part of a well-water treatment plan, but it is not automatically required for every private well and it is not a substitute for testing, well maintenance or corrective work.

The sensible starting point is to understand the source water, confirm bacteriological results through a qualified laboratory and check whether the water is suitable for UV treatment.

What does UV treatment do?

A UV disinfection system passes water through a stainless-steel chamber containing an ultraviolet lamp. The UV energy can inactivate bacteria, viruses and other microorganisms as water passes through the chamber by damaging their ability to reproduce.

UV treatment works at the point where the chamber is installed. In a typical whole-home system, it is installed near the end of the treatment sequence before water enters the household plumbing. UV does not add a disinfectant residual, so it does not continue protecting plumbing after the water leaves the chamber.

What UV does not remove or correct

UV is a disinfection process, not a general-purpose water filter. It does not remove:

  • Minerals or dissolved salts
  • Hardness
  • Iron or manganese
  • Sediment
  • Arsenic or nitrate
  • Tannins or other colour-causing compounds
  • Chemical contaminants
  • Hydrogen sulfide odour

It also does not correct contamination introduced downstream of the UV chamber. A damaged well cap, poor drainage, surface-water entry, contaminated storage or downstream plumbing still needs to be investigated and corrected where possible.

When might UV be appropriate for a private well?

UV may be considered when:

  • A properly collected sample shows E. coli or another microbiological concern
  • Total coliform results recur after corrective work
  • The well is shallow, vulnerable to surface influence or difficult to protect
  • The source has a history of intermittent contamination
  • A health authority or qualified professional recommends continuous disinfection
  • An existing UV system needs to be replaced, resized or returned to service

A private well with consistently acceptable test results, sound construction and low contamination risk may not require continuous UV. The decision should follow the available evidence rather than the simple fact that a property uses a well.

Start with bacteria testing

HealthLink BC recommends bacteriological testing for private wells at least once per year and immediately if you notice a change in clarity, colour or taste. Retesting is also sensible after flooding, heavy rainfall, well repairs or other events that may affect the source.

The two common indicators are total coliforms and E. coli. Total coliforms can indicate sanitation or surface-influence concerns. E. coli can indicate fecal contamination and HealthLink BC identifies it as an immediate health concern.

If E. coli is detected, do not rely on the water for drinking, food preparation or brushing teeth until you have followed public-health guidance and have an appropriate safe-water solution.

Why pretreatment matters before UV

UV performance depends on how much light can pass through the water. Cloudiness, suspended particles, colour and deposits on the quartz sleeve can reduce the UV dose reaching microorganisms. Particles can also shield microorganisms from the light, which is why cartridge or other sediment prefiltration is commonly installed ahead of the UV chamber.

Depending on the test results, pretreatment may also be needed for iron, manganese, hardness, tannins, colour, turbidity or other substances that foul the sleeve or absorb UV light. The treatment sequence should be based on the water entering the chamber, not just the equipment available in a catalogue.

Size the system for peak flow

A UV system should be matched to the household's actual peak flow rate and the water's UV transmittance. The number of bedrooms or the size of the house is not enough information by itself. Consider simultaneous demand from showers, toilets, laundry, faucets and outdoor use.

If flow exceeds the unit's rated or certified capacity, water may move through the chamber too quickly for the intended UV dose. For microbiological applications, ask whether the proposed unit is certified or validated for the intended use. NSF/ANSI 55 Class A systems are intended for disinfection of contaminated water under their specified operating conditions; Class B systems are a different category.

Use shock chlorination for corrective work when appropriate

Shock chlorination may be part of investigating or correcting bacterial contamination at a private well. It can help address contamination in the well and plumbing, but it does not replace repairs to a damaged well cap, poor drainage, surface-water entry or another underlying source problem.

It also does not provide ongoing protection after the chlorine has been flushed away. Where contamination recurs or the source remains vulnerable, continuous disinfection such as UV may be considered as part of a longer-term plan. After corrective work, the water should be retested according to public-health or laboratory guidance.

Maintain the UV system on schedule

A UV lamp may still appear lit after its useful disinfection output has declined. Lamp replacement is therefore typically scheduled annually or according to the equipment manufacturer's instructions.

  • Replace the lamp on schedule
  • Clean the quartz sleeve
  • Replace sediment filters
  • Check seals and connections
  • Review UV intensity or dose readings where available
  • Test alarms and shutoff functions where provided
  • Confirm flow remains within the rated range
  • Retest the water as appropriate

Troubleshooting an existing UV system

  1. Check the alarm. A low-intensity, lamp-age or flow alarm requires attention even if the lamp is visibly on.
  2. Confirm lamp age. An overdue lamp may provide light without the intended output.
  3. Inspect the quartz sleeve. Scale or deposits can block UV transmission.
  4. Check pretreatment. Missing, clogged or unsuitable prefiltration can affect clarity, flow and sleeve condition.
  5. Review flow. Excessive demand or an incorrect bypass position can exceed the rated flow.
  6. Inspect the well and plumbing. UV cannot correct contamination entering downstream or recurring at the source.
  7. Retest the water. Field observations cannot confirm whether bacteria are present or whether treatment is performing microbiologically.

A practical UV treatment planning process

  1. Describe the concern, test result, timing, well type and recent repairs or weather events.
  2. Test the water for total coliforms and E. coli, with additional chemical testing where appropriate.
  3. Inspect the source, wellhead, drainage, pressure system, storage and plumbing.
  4. Assess sediment, turbidity, iron, manganese, hardness, colour and other factors that affect UV performance.
  5. Select and size the system for the water conditions and peak flow.
  6. Install and verify the plumbing sequence, alarms and service access.
  7. Maintain the system and continue periodic testing.

Frequently asked questions

Does every private well on Vancouver Island need UV?

No. UV may be appropriate for confirmed or recurring microbiological concerns, a vulnerable source or an existing disinfection requirement. A well with good test results and sound protection may not require it.

Can UV make well water safe if the lamp is on?

Not by itself. Performance depends on flow, water clarity, UV transmittance, lamp age, sleeve condition, pretreatment and alarm operation. A lamp that appears lit is not proof that the intended UV dose is being delivered.

Does UV remove iron, hardness or sulfur odour?

No. UV inactivates microorganisms. Iron, hardness and odour require separate assessment and may need different treatment.

How often should private-well water be tested?

HealthLink BC recommends bacteriological testing at least annually and immediately after changes in clarity, colour or taste, with retesting after events that may affect the source.
