Diagnose Before You Buy: Why Water Problems Should Be Identified Before Equipment

A practical troubleshooting sequence for identifying the cause of water stains, odours, low pressure or treatment problems before buying replacement equipment.

Published November 17, 2026 · Brian Paul, Pure Water Pros

Orange stains, cloudy water, rotten-egg odour, scale and unpleasant taste can all prompt the same question: “What equipment should I buy?” The more useful first question is: “What is causing the problem?”

Water-treatment equipment works best when it is selected around the source water, test results, plumbing and actual goal. Buying a filter or softener from the symptom alone can leave the original problem unchanged and create new maintenance costs.

Similar symptoms can have different causes

A visual clue is useful, but it is not a complete diagnosis.

  • Orange or brown staining may involve dissolved iron, iron particles, corrosion or iron bacteria.
  • Dark staining may involve manganese, but colour alone cannot confirm it.
  • Tea-coloured water may come from natural organic matter such as tannins, disturbed sediment or another source.
  • Rotten-egg odour may involve hydrogen sulphide in the source water, a water heater reaction or bacterial activity.
  • White scale may involve hardness, while white spots on very soft municipal water may simply be dried dissolved solids or cleaning residue.
  • Cloudiness can come from air, sediment, treatment media or a plumbing condition.
  • Low pressure can come from a clogged cartridge, a pressure-system problem, restricted plumbing or an undersized treatment component.

The correct treatment for one cause may do little for another.

Start with the water source

First identify whether the property uses:

  • Greater Victoria municipal water;
  • a private well;
  • a shared well or small water system;
  • rainwater storage;
  • hauled water; or
  • more than one source.

Municipal reports provide useful system-level information, but they cannot diagnose a building's plumbing. A private well needs property-specific testing because neighbouring wells can have different construction, geology and water quality.

Separate where and when the problem occurs

Before testing or replacing equipment, make a few comparisons:

  1. Is the issue at every cold tap or only one fixture?
  2. Does it occur only in hot water?
  3. Did it begin after plumbing work, filter replacement, heavy rain or well service?
  4. Is it constant or seasonal?
  5. Does it improve after the water runs?
  6. Is there existing equipment, and what changes before and after it?
  7. Has flow or pressure changed?

Hot-water-only odour points the investigation toward the water heater. A single discoloured faucet may be a local fixture or branch-line issue. A whole-home change after a storm may justify well inspection and testing.

Test for what could change the decision

A useful test plan is not necessarily the largest panel available. Choose parameters that can confirm safety, distinguish likely causes or determine treatment suitability.

For a private well, HealthLink BC recommends bacteriological testing at least once per year and chemical testing on an ongoing annual basis. Testing should also be repeated after events that may affect water quality. Use a qualified laboratory and follow its sampling instructions.

Depending on the concern, useful measurements may include:

  • total coliforms and E. coli;
  • iron and manganese;
  • hardness;
  • pH and alkalinity;
  • turbidity or suspended material;
  • sulphide-related testing;
  • tannins or colour;
  • total dissolved solids; and
  • a contaminant-specific test recommended by a laboratory or health authority.

A free hardness strip or handheld total-dissolved-solids meter cannot establish that water is microbiologically safe and cannot identify most individual contaminants.

Inspect the source and plumbing

Testing describes the sample. Inspection helps explain why the result or symptom exists.

For a private well, examine the well cap, grading, drainage, recent flooding, pressure tank, storage, visible leaks and any route for surface water to enter. If bacteria are present, correct source vulnerabilities where possible instead of treating UV as a substitute for well repairs.

Inside the building, inspect bypass positions, cartridge condition, water-heater history, corrosion, unused branches and treatment order. A clogged prefilter can reduce pressure. An exhausted carbon cartridge can affect taste. A softener that is bypassed or not drawing brine will not soften, even if its controller appears normal.

Confirm the required flow rate

Treatment equipment must handle the home's peak flow without excessive pressure loss. Sizing only by bedroom count or average daily consumption can miss simultaneous demand from showers, toilets, laundry and faucets.

Measure or estimate realistic peak flow and compare it with the equipment's service flow, backwash requirements and plumbing size. This is especially important for UV chambers, iron filters, carbon tanks and other whole-home systems.

Put treatment in the correct order

When more than one condition exists, order matters. Examples include:

  • sediment control before equipment that can plug or foul;
  • iron or manganese treatment before a UV chamber when those parameters exceed the UV manufacturer's inlet limits;
  • adequate pretreatment before fine cartridges;
  • disinfection placed after the treatment stages that could shield microorganisms; and
  • an unsoftened drinking-water tap when appropriate for the system and household.

There is no universal equipment train for every well. The water and goal determine the sequence.

Diagnose existing equipment before replacing it

If a system once worked, replacement should not be the first assumption. Check:

  • whether it is in service or bypass;
  • inlet and outlet pressure;
  • filter differential pressure;
  • salt or chemical use;
  • regeneration or backwash history;
  • lamp age and alarms;
  • valve programming;
  • drain flow;
  • media age and fouling; and
  • a before-and-after water sample where appropriate.

The fault may be a blocked cartridge, incorrect setting, salt bridge, failed injector, fouled sleeve or plumbing restriction rather than an entire system that needs replacement.

Greater Victoria municipal water is generally very soft

The CRD reports median hardness of about 17.4 mg/L as calcium carbonate. For most homes on the regional municipal supply, that does not justify a conventional softener.

If someone recommends a softener for a municipal-water home, ask what measured hardness or specific problem it is intended to solve. Scale-like residue, spots or dry skin do not by themselves prove that hardness is the cause.

A practical diagnosis sequence

  1. Define the water source and exact symptom.
  2. Compare hot versus cold and one fixture versus the whole home.
  3. Review recent weather, plumbing and service changes.
  4. Obtain the safety and chemistry tests that can change the decision.
  5. Inspect the source, plumbing and existing equipment.
  6. Confirm peak flow, pressure and drain requirements.
  7. Select treatment only after the cause and goal are clear.
  8. Verify the result after service or installation.

Frequently asked questions

Can a photo identify the right treatment system?

A photo can reveal equipment, staining or plumbing clues, but it cannot measure bacteria, iron, manganese, hardness or other water chemistry.

Do I need a full laboratory panel for every problem?

Not always. The panel should cover safety requirements and the parameters that could change the diagnosis. A qualified laboratory or health authority can help identify appropriate tests.

Should old equipment always be replaced?

No. Diagnose bypass position, flow, settings, maintenance and before-and-after performance first. Repair or service may be more appropriate.

Is a water softener a general whole-home filter?

No. A softener primarily exchanges hardness minerals and may handle limited dissolved iron only within its documented limits. It is not a substitute for microbiological, sediment, chemical or odour treatment.
