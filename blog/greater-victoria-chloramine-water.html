Why Does Greater Victoria Tap Water Taste or Smell Like Chlorine? What Homeowners Should Know About Chloramine

A practical, non-alarmist look at why Greater Victoria tap water can taste or smell like chlorine, what chloramine does, and when filtration may — or may not — make sense.

Published September 10, 2026 · Pure Water Pros

If your tap water in Victoria, Langford, Colwood, View Royal or another Greater Victoria municipality tastes or smells slightly like chlorine, you are not necessarily dealing with a water-quality problem.

The Capital Regional District (CRD) intentionally uses chloramine as part of its drinking-water disinfection process. The CRD states that Greater Victoria’s municipal water is safe to drink and meets provincial and federal health-based regulations.

Some homeowners still choose filtration because they prefer a different taste or odour. That is a comfort and preference decision: not a requirement because the municipal water is unsafe.

What is chloramine, and why is it in Greater Victoria’s water?

The CRD uses a multiple-barrier approach to protect the region’s drinking water. Treatment includes ultraviolet disinfection, chlorine and ammonia.

According to the CRD, chlorine is added first. A small amount of ammonia is then added, combining with chlorine to form chloramine. This disinfectant remains more stable as water travels through the distribution system.

That stability is important because drinking water moves through many kilometres of water mains before reaching your home. A lasting disinfectant residual helps protect the water during distribution.

The CRD describes chloramine concentrations in Greater Victoria’s drinking water as below 3.0 mg/L and states that chloramines have no harmful effect on the human body at the concentrations used in the system. You can review the CRD’s explanation on its Drinking Water Quality page.

Why can you still notice a chlorine taste or smell?

Taste and odour are not always the same as safety. People can notice disinfectant taste or odour at levels that remain within regulated and monitored ranges.

Several factors may make the taste or smell more noticeable:

  • Seasonal water-main flushing: Greater Victoria municipalities flush water mains during fall and winter. Changes in the pipes can temporarily affect how the water tastes or smells.
  • Warmer water: Water temperature can affect how noticeable different tastes and odours are.
  • Water movement and demand: Changes in water use or flow through the mains may make the disinfectant more noticeable.
  • Water that has been sitting in household plumbing: Water left in pipes for a long time may taste different when first drawn.
  • Local plumbing or fixtures: An odour that occurs at only one tap may come from the faucet, supply line or drain rather than the municipal supply.
  • Individual sensitivity: Some people notice chloramine more readily than others.

The CRD notes that taste and odour can also change naturally through the year. If a concern is widespread, persistent or significantly different from normal, contact the relevant municipality or the CRD rather than assuming a filter is the correct first step.

Greater Victoria water is very soft

Greater Victoria’s municipal water is also very soft. The CRD reports a median hardness of approximately 17.4 mg/L as calcium carbonate.

Hardness refers mainly to dissolved calcium and magnesium. At this level, Greater Victoria water is not typically associated with the heavy scale problems found in hard-water regions.

This means a water softener is usually not the first answer to a chlorine or chloramine taste. A softener is intended to address hardness-related concerns such as scale, spotting and poor soap performance. It does not serve the same purpose as carbon filtration.

If your main concern is disinfectant taste or odour, start by identifying that concern separately from hardness.

Do you need a filter for chloramine?

No. Greater Victoria municipal water is treated and monitored by the CRD. Some homeowners still choose filtration for taste or odour preference.

You may still choose filtration for practical reasons, such as:

  • Preferring a milder taste or odour
  • Wanting filtered water at a kitchen tap
  • Reducing the taste of disinfectant in drinking and cooking water
  • Improving the taste of water used for coffee, tea or ice
  • Filtering water throughout the home for personal preference

The appropriate system depends on where you notice the issue and what result you want.

Standard activated carbon

Activated carbon can reduce chlorine-related taste and odour when the filter is correctly selected and maintained. It is commonly used in pitcher filters, under-sink cartridges and whole-home filtration systems.

Not all carbon filters perform the same way with chloramine. “Carbon” is a broad category, not a verified result. Chloramine reduction is a specific performance claim under NSF/ANSI 42, so equipment should be selected based on verified performance, flow rate, capacity and maintenance requirements rather than simply being labelled carbon.

Carbon performance depends on factors such as:

  • Verified chloramine-reduction performance
  • Water flow rate
  • Contact time with the media
  • Cartridge or vessel size and type
  • Amount of water used
  • Replacement schedule
  • The specific disinfectant being treated

A small cartridge may be suitable for a drinking-water tap but not for the flow demands of an entire home.

Catalytic carbon

Catalytic carbon is a modified carbon media intended to improve chloramine reduction compared with many standard carbon products. It can be considered when a homeowner wants to reduce chloramine taste or odour at more than one tap.

However, the media name alone does not determine whether the system will perform well. As with other carbon-based treatment, verified performance, media volume, vessel size, flow rate, contact time and maintenance planning all matter.

A catalytic-carbon system may be appropriate for a whole-home application where the goal is to reduce taste and odour at showers, sinks and other fixtures. In another home, an under-sink filter may be the more practical choice if the concern is limited to drinking and cooking water.

Whole-home filtration or drinking-water filtration?

The location of the concern is an important part of the decision.

Choose point-of-use filtration when:

  • You mainly notice the taste in drinking water
  • You want filtered water at one kitchen faucet
  • You do not want to treat water used for bathing or laundry
  • Your household’s drinking-water demand is modest

An under-sink system can provide treated water at a dedicated drinking-water faucet. Reverse osmosis may be considered when you have broader drinking-water goals, but it is more involved than a simple carbon filter and requires appropriate pressure, pretreatment and ongoing maintenance.

Consider whole-home filtration when:

  • You notice the taste or odour at several fixtures
  • You want to reduce the disinfectant taste in bathing water
  • You prefer treated water throughout the plumbing system
  • The property has flow and installation conditions that support the system

Whole-home filtration must be sized around household flow. A system that reduces taste at a low-flow kitchen faucet may not be suitable for simultaneous showers, toilets, laundry and other demands.

Health Canada does not consider chloramine removal necessary for health reasons and notes that removing the disinfectant residual can reduce the protection provided in drinking-water plumbing. Homeowners may still choose appropriately designed treatment for aesthetic preferences, but whole-home equipment needs proper sizing, installation and maintenance.

A whole-home carbon or catalytic-carbon system also requires planned maintenance. Media may need replacement or service based on usage, water conditions and the equipment design.

What filtration can and cannot do

A carbon or catalytic-carbon system may help with chlorine or chloramine taste and odour where the system is correctly selected.

It does not automatically:

  • Remove every contaminant
  • Disinfect water
  • Correct low water pressure
  • Address bacteria in a private well
  • Remove iron or sulfur odour from well water
  • Solve plumbing or water-heater problems
  • Replace routine filter or media maintenance
  • Make an incorrectly sized system perform properly

This distinction matters because different water problems require different treatment categories. For example, sediment filtration, softening, carbon treatment, reverse osmosis and UV disinfection are not interchangeable.

A practical way to check the problem

Before choosing equipment, work through the concern in order.

  1. Describe the change: Note whether the taste or odour occurs at every tap or only one fixture. Record whether it affects cold water, hot water or both.
  2. Check the timing: Consider whether the issue began after water-main flushing, plumbing work, a change in household occupancy or a period when the water was unused.
  3. Compare locations: Fill a clean glass from more than one cold-water tap. If the issue occurs at only one fixture, inspect the faucet, drain and local plumbing before assuming the municipal water is the cause.
  4. Choose the treatment goal: Decide whether you want better drinking-water taste, treatment at one tap or reduced taste and odour throughout the home.
  5. Select and maintain the system: Match the filter type, size and flow rating to the goal. Follow the manufacturer's replacement schedule and arrange service if pressure loss, leaks or changes in taste occur.

If the water is discoloured, has a sudden unusual odour, or the concern continues across the home, contact your water supplier. A filtration company can help assess equipment and household plumbing, but it does not replace the CRD or municipal water-quality authority.

Frequently asked questions

Is Greater Victoria tap water safe to drink?

Yes. The CRD states that Greater Victoria’s drinking water meets provincial and federal health-based regulations.

Is chloramine the same as chlorine?

No. Chloramine forms when chlorine combines with ammonia. It is used because it remains more stable in the distribution system. People may still describe the resulting taste or odour as “chlorine.”

Does a chlorine smell mean the water is unsafe?

Not necessarily. Taste and odour are separate from health-based water-quality limits. If the change is sudden, strong, widespread or persistent, contact the CRD or your municipality for guidance.

Will a water softener remove chloramine?

A water softener is designed to reduce hardness minerals. It is not the normal treatment for chlorine or chloramine taste and odour.

Is reverse osmosis required for Greater Victoria tap water?

No. Reverse osmosis is not required for Greater Victoria municipal water. It may be selected for specific drinking-water preferences or treatment goals after the water, pressure, space and maintenance requirements are reviewed.
