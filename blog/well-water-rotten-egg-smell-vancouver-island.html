Why Does My Well Water Smell Like Rotten Eggs? Sulfur Odour on Vancouver Island Explained

A practical guide to separating water-heater odour from a whole-well sulfur problem, deciding what to test, and choosing treatment only after the water and system conditions are understood.

Published September 10, 2026 · Brian Paul, Pure Water Pros

A rotten-egg smell from your well water is unpleasant, but the odour does not point to one single cause. It may come from dissolved hydrogen sulfide, sulfur-reducing bacteria, the water heater, or a combination of water chemistry and equipment conditions.

The first useful question is simple: Does the odour occur only in hot water, or in both hot and cold water?

That distinction helps determine whether you should start with the water heater or investigate the well and plumbing system. Before choosing a filter, softener or chemical system, the water source and site conditions need to be checked.

What causes a rotten-egg smell in well water?

The odour is commonly associated with hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), a dissolved gas that can smell strongly at low concentrations. Hydrogen sulfide may be present naturally in groundwater, or it may be produced when sulfur-reducing bacteria convert sulfate into sulfide in low-oxygen parts of a well or plumbing system.

Other possible sources include:

  • Sulfur bacteria in the well, pressure tank or plumbing.
  • A reaction involving the magnesium anode rod inside the water heater.
  • Iron or manganese interacting with sulfur compounds.
  • Stagnant water or localized bacterial growth in a section of plumbing.
  • Water chemistry that affects how well a treatment system operates.

Hydrogen sulfide is generally an aesthetic concern at the levels commonly found in household water, causing odour, taste and possible corrosion. However, a new or unusually strong odour should not be dismissed without checking the source. Hydrogen sulfide gas can also be hazardous in poorly ventilated, confined spaces such as well pits. Do not enter a well pit or enclosed pump area to investigate the smell.

The National Ground Water Association provides additional background in its guide to hydrogen sulfide in private well water.

Check whether the smell is hot-only or hot and cold

This is the most useful first troubleshooting step for a homeowner.

Smell only in hot water

If the cold water is odour-free but the hot water smells, the water heater is a likely source. The magnesium anode rod used to protect the tank from corrosion can react with sulfate in the water. Sulfur bacteria may also grow inside the heater.

In this situation, a whole-home sulfur filter may not be necessary. A qualified plumber or water-heater technician can assess options such as:

  • Inspecting or replacing the magnesium anode rod.
  • Using an approved alternative anode where suitable.
  • Flushing and disinfecting the heater.
  • Checking the heater manufacturer’s requirements and warranty.
  • Reviewing the operating temperature and scalding risk.

Do not simply remove the anode rod without considering the effect on tank corrosion protection. The correct replacement depends on the heater design and manufacturer guidance.

Smell in both hot and cold water

If the odour is present in cold water at several fixtures, including an outside tap, the well, pressure tank or plumbing system is more likely to be involved.

Check more than one location:

  • A kitchen cold-water tap.
  • A bathroom cold-water tap.
  • An outdoor hose bib, if it is connected before treatment equipment.
  • Water that has been sitting in the pipes overnight.
  • Water before and after any existing filter or softener.

If the smell is limited to one fixture, the cause may be local to that faucet or branch line. If it is present throughout the home, the assessment should include the well water, equipment and plumbing layout.

What should be tested?

When the odour affects cold water or keeps returning, testing can help separate a temporary bacterial issue from a water chemistry problem.

Depending on the situation, testing may include:

  • Hydrogen sulfide or sulfide, using appropriate sampling procedures.
  • Sulfate.
  • Iron and manganese.
  • pH and alkalinity.
  • Hardness.
  • Total coliform and E. coli bacteria where indicated.
  • Other parameters based on the well, property history and symptoms.

Total coliform and E. coli testing helps assess microbiological water safety, but it does not diagnose sulfur-reducing bacteria. A rotten-egg odour, staining or slime can suggest biological activity, but those signs alone do not confirm sulfur bacteria.

Hydrogen sulfide can escape from a water sample during transport. Follow the laboratory’s instructions carefully, and ask whether the test needs to be collected or stabilized in a particular way.

Testing is not just about finding a number. The results need to be considered alongside:

  • Well yield and recovery rate.
  • Pump size and available flow.
  • Peak household flow demand.
  • Pressure-tank settings.
  • Drain capacity for backwashing equipment.
  • Available installation space.
  • Existing treatment equipment.
  • The water’s iron, manganese and pH levels.

This is why the same treatment does not work equally well for every private well on Vancouver Island.

Could shock chlorination help?

If sulfur bacteria are suspected in the well or plumbing, shock chlorination may reduce the odour. It can be useful as a corrective or diagnostic step, but it is not always a permanent solution.

Shock chlorination may:

  • Reduce sulfur bacteria in the well and household plumbing.
  • Temporarily improve a rotten-egg smell.
  • Help show whether bacteria are contributing to the problem.

The odour may return if bacteria remain in the formation, pressure tank or biofilm inside the system. Chlorination also needs to be done carefully, with the correct amount, contact time and flushing procedure. Improper dosing can damage equipment, leave chlorine in the plumbing or create a discharge problem.

For detailed general guidance, see Penn State Extension’s information on hydrogen sulfide in water wells and shock chlorination of wells and springs.

Treatment options for sulfur odour

The appropriate option depends on the test results and the way the water system operates. Common approaches include the following.

Activated or catalytic carbon

Carbon may help with lower levels of hydrogen sulfide and can also improve taste and odour after another treatment step. Catalytic carbon is different from basic activated carbon and may be selected for more demanding applications.

Carbon is not automatically the right choice for strong or persistent odour. It can become exhausted, support bacterial growth when poorly maintained and require regular replacement or backwashing, depending on the system.

Air injection or aeration

Air-injection oxidation systems introduce air into the water to help convert or release hydrogen sulfide before filtration. They may be suitable for selected low-to-moderate conditions, but performance depends on concentration, pH, iron, manganese, dissolved oxygen, flow and the filter media used.

An air-injection system that works on one well may underperform on another if the water chemistry or flow conditions are different.

Oxidizing filtration

Oxidizing media can convert hydrogen sulfide, iron and manganese into particles that the filter can capture. Some systems use specialized media and automatic backwashing.

These systems can be useful when odour occurs with staining or metallic taste, but they need suitable flow for backwashing and regular service. Media selection should follow testing rather than the symptom alone.

Chemical injection and contact tanks

Hydrogen peroxide injection may be considered as an oxidation option for stronger hydrogen sulfide, or where iron and manganese also need to be addressed. These systems usually require:

  • A properly sized dosing pump.
  • A contact or retention tank.
  • A filter for oxidized particles and residual treatment chemicals.
  • Chemical storage and regular refilling.
  • Monitoring and maintenance.

They can be effective, but they are more involved than installing a basic cartridge filter. The system must be designed around the actual water and household demand.

If microbiological contamination is present, that needs to be addressed separately with an appropriately designed disinfection treatment approach. Oxidation for odour or metals should not be treated as the same decision as primary disinfection.

A softener

A conventional water softener is not a primary treatment for hydrogen sulfide odour. It may be appropriate if testing confirms hard water, but it should not be selected simply because the water smells like rotten eggs.

Likewise, an iron filter may help when iron and sulfur occur together, but not every iron filter is designed for the same hydrogen sulfide conditions.

A practical troubleshooting path

Use this sequence before purchasing equipment:

  1. Describe the pattern.
    Note whether the odour is hot-only, cold-only or present in both hot and cold water.

  2. Compare several fixtures.
    Check indoor taps and an outside tap where possible. Note whether the smell is limited to one area.

  3. Review existing equipment.
    Record any softener, filter, UV system, chemical pump or older equipment, including recent changes in operation.

  4. Test where appropriate.
    Include hydrogen sulfide or sulfide, bacteria and related water chemistry based on the symptoms.

  5. Check the system conditions.
    Flow rate, pressure, well yield, drain capacity and available space can limit which treatment options are suitable.

  6. Choose the treatment category.
    The result may be heater service, shock chlorination, oxidation and filtration, chemical injection, or no whole-home treatment at all.

  7. Verify and maintain the system.
    After installation, confirm that the odour and related symptoms have improved. Continue filter changes, media service, chemical replenishment and testing as required.

Start with the source of the odour, not a product label.
