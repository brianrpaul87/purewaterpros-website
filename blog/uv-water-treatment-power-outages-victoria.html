UV Water Treatment and Power Outages: What Homeowners Should Know

What a power outage means for a private-well UV system, what to do when power returns, and why the right response depends on whether water was used while treatment was off.

Published November 12, 2026 · Brian Paul, Pure Water Pros

A whole-home ultraviolet system disinfects water only while the lamp and controller are operating. During a power outage the well pump may also stop, but that does not make every outage a contamination event. The important question is whether water moved through or around the UV chamber while the system was unable to treat it.

If no water was used, untreated water normally did not move into the downstream plumbing. If water was drawn from a pressure tank, storage tank or bypass while the UV system was off, untreated water may have entered the plumbing. That calls for a more careful restart instead of assuming that a few minutes of flushing proves the system is safe.

What a UV system does—and does not do

UV systems expose flowing water to ultraviolet light inside a treatment chamber. When the equipment is correctly selected, installed and maintained, that light can inactivate microorganisms under the system's specified operating conditions.

UV does not add a disinfectant residual. Once water leaves the chamber, there is no continuing UV protection in the pipes. UV also does not remove sediment, iron, manganese, hardness, colour or chemical contaminants. Those conditions may need separate testing or pretreatment because they can interfere with UV performance or foul the quartz sleeve.

What happens when the power goes out?

The UV lamp and its monitoring system stop when electrical power is unavailable. The response depends on what happened during the interruption:

  • No water was used: water generally stayed in place, so untreated water was not pulled through the UV chamber simply because the power failed.
  • Water was used: stored pressure or water from another source may have moved water past an unpowered UV chamber and into the downstream plumbing.
  • A bypass was open: water may have gone around treatment altogether. Treat that as a separate plumbing and water-quality concern.
  • An alarm or fault remains after power returns: do not assume treatment has resumed until the controller indicates normal operation and any model-specific checks are complete.

During an outage

  1. Avoid using the treated-water fixtures if practical. This reduces the chance of drawing untreated water into the downstream plumbing.
  2. Do not use the UV bypass for normal household water. A bypass is not disinfection.
  3. Use a known safe supply for drinking and food preparation if you need water before treatment is available again.
  4. Check the equipment manual. Backup-power arrangements, shutoff valves, alarms and restart steps vary by system.

A generator or battery-backed arrangement can keep some systems operating, but only when it is installed and used within the electrical and equipment requirements. Never improvise around the controller, safety ground, alarms or automatic shutoff equipment.

When power returns

  1. Keep water off during the restart period specified for your equipment. For example, current VIQUA instructions for several residential systems say not to pass water through the UV system for at least five minutes after power is applied, including after an interruption.
  2. Confirm normal controller status. Check for lamp, controller, low-UV or other alarms. A visible lamp alone does not confirm the intended treatment conditions.
  3. Consider whether water was used while the lamp was off. If it was, follow the manufacturer's restart and disinfection instructions for the specific system. Depending on the equipment, plumbing and risk, downstream disinfection and follow-up testing may be appropriate.
  4. Address recurring outages or faults. Frequent interruptions, nuisance alarms, failed lamps or unreliable power deserve diagnosis rather than repeated resets.

Do not rely on a generic instruction to “run every tap for a few minutes.” Flushing may be part of a model-specific procedure, but it does not by itself verify that downstream plumbing is microbiologically safe.

When should you test the water?

Private-well owners remain responsible for testing their water. HealthLink BC recommends bacteriological testing at least annually and retesting after events that may affect water quality. Testing is especially sensible after a suspected contamination event, flooding, well work, an extended treatment interruption or a positive result.

If a test detects E. coli, HealthLink BC treats that as an immediate health concern. Use an appropriate safe-water alternative and follow public-health or qualified-laboratory guidance while the source and treatment system are investigated.

Maintenance still matters after the outage

A power interruption does not reset lamp age or clean the quartz sleeve. Keep the system on its model-specific maintenance schedule:

  • replace the lamp or lamp unit at the interval specified by its manufacturer;
  • inspect and clean the quartz sleeve as required;
  • replace installed prefilters on condition and schedule;
  • check seals, alarms and any automatic shutoff equipment;
  • confirm that flow and incoming water quality remain within the system's limits; and
  • continue periodic well-water testing.

Many conventional residential UV lamps are rated around 9,000 hours and are replaced annually in full-time homes, but newer models and lamp units can have different intervals. Use the controller and manual for the exact system rather than a universal anniversary rule.

Municipal water is a different situation

Most Greater Victoria homes receive CRD municipal water that is already disinfected and monitored. A private-well UV outage article should not be read as a reason for every municipal-water home to add UV equipment. Treatment should follow the water source, test results and a defined problem.

Frequently asked questions

Does every UV outage contaminate the plumbing?

No. An outage stops UV treatment, but it does not automatically prove that the well or plumbing became contaminated. If water was used while UV was off, untreated water may have entered downstream plumbing and the restart should be handled accordingly.

Can I tell that treatment is working because the lamp is glowing?

No. Lamp age, sleeve condition, flow, water clarity, UV transmittance and alarms all matter. Follow the controller and manual for the installed system.

Should I turn my UV system off when no water is being used?

Normally no. VIQUA advises leaving its systems on during ordinary use. If a system is being drained or winterized, follow its specific instructions.
