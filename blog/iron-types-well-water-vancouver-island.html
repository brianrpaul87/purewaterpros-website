Iron in Well Water: Why the Type of Iron Changes the Fix

Clear-water iron, rust-coloured particles and iron bacteria require different investigation and treatment decisions.

Published November 19, 2026 · Brian Paul, Pure Water Pros

Iron in private-well water is often described as one problem, but it can appear in different forms. Water may leave the tap clear and turn orange later, arrive with visible rust-coloured particles, or carry slippery deposits associated with iron bacteria. The correct investigation and treatment depend on which condition is present and what else is in the water.

That is why “install an iron filter” is not a complete diagnosis.

Dissolved ferrous iron: clear water that changes later

Ferrous iron is dissolved in the water. A sample can look clear when it is first drawn, then turn yellow, orange or reddish-brown as oxygen converts the iron to a less soluble form.

Typical clues include:

  • clear water at first;
  • colour developing after standing;
  • orange staining in toilets, sinks or laundry; and
  • iron deposits forming after aeration or contact with an oxidant.

Treatment usually requires converting the dissolved iron into particles and then filtering those particles. The right oxidation and filtration method depends on iron concentration, pH, alkalinity, oxygen demand, flow and other water chemistry.

Ferric or particulate iron: visible colour or particles at the tap

Ferric iron is already oxidized and may appear as orange-brown water, flakes or sediment as soon as it is drawn.

Before selecting treatment, determine whether the particles originate from:

  • the aquifer or well;
  • corrosion in steel components or plumbing;
  • an old pressure tank;
  • a treatment unit releasing accumulated material; or
  • disturbed sediment after service work.

Particulate iron may be controlled with sediment filtration, but filter size and type should follow particle size, loading and required flow. A fine cartridge installed without understanding the load can plug rapidly and reduce household pressure.

Iron bacteria: slime, fouling and recurring deposits

Iron bacteria use iron in their growth processes and can form orange, brown or yellowish slime. They may cause swampy, musty or petroleum-like odours, oily-looking films and recurring fouling in wells, plumbing, toilets and treatment equipment.

Iron bacteria are not the same as E. coli and their presence is not established by a standard potability test. A qualified laboratory or well professional may recommend specific sampling and investigation.

Treatment at the house can remove some iron or slime from the water after it leaves the well, but it does not necessarily control a colony established inside the well. Severe or recurring cases may require professional well cleaning, disinfection and rehabilitation. Pennsylvania State University Extension notes that established colonies can be difficult to eradicate and may require combined physical and chemical control.

Do not mix chlorine and acid treatments. Well rehabilitation and high-strength disinfection are professional work.

Manganese and tannins can complicate the picture

Manganese can produce dark brown or black staining and may occur with iron. Health Canada has a health-based maximum acceptable concentration and a lower aesthetic objective for manganese, so a laboratory result should be interpreted using current Canadian guidance rather than colour alone.

Natural organic matter such as tannins can give water a tea-like colour. Tannins are not simply another type of iron, and treatment that works for iron may not correct organic colour.

A complete investigation may therefore include iron, manganese, colour or tannin-related testing rather than relying on a single visual clue.

Test before choosing equipment

A useful laboratory plan may include:

  • total iron;
  • dissolved iron where the laboratory offers an appropriate method;
  • manganese;
  • pH and alkalinity;
  • hardness;
  • turbidity or suspended solids;
  • colour or tannin-related analysis where indicated;
  • sulphide-related testing if odour is present; and
  • total coliforms and E. coli for private-well safety.

The sample's appearance and timing still matter. Tell the laboratory whether water is clear at first, changes after standing or contains particles immediately. If corrosion is suspected, compare a source sample with samples after the pressure system or plumbing.

HealthLink BC recommends regular bacteriological and chemical testing for private wells through a qualified laboratory. An iron test does not replace microbiological testing.

Why pH and flow matter

Many iron-treatment processes depend on oxidation. Reaction time and media performance change with pH, dissolved oxygen and competing substances. Manganese is often more difficult to oxidize than iron.

A treatment tank must also handle the home's service flow and have enough drain flow for backwashing. An undersized unit or inadequate backwash can lead to pressure loss, media fouling and iron carryover even when the basic treatment method is reasonable.

Common treatment approaches

Sediment filtration

Appropriate when the problem is already particulate and the filter is sized for the particle load and household flow. It does not remove significant dissolved iron that passes through in solution.

Oxidation followed by filtration

Often used for dissolved iron. Air, chlorine or another documented oxidant converts iron so a filter can capture it. The exact method should follow water chemistry and equipment specifications.

Backwashing iron media

Specialized media may oxidize, adsorb or filter iron. Each product has limits for pH, iron, manganese, sulphide, flow and backwash. “Iron filter” is a category, not a universal specification.

Water softening

A softener may remove a limited amount of dissolved iron when hardness and other conditions fit the manufacturer's limits. It is not a general solution for heavy iron, particles or iron bacteria, and iron can foul resin.

Source and well work

If the problem originates from corrosion, surface entry, a damaged well component or an established iron-bacteria colony, correcting the well or plumbing may be more important than adding another tank.

Diagnose the existing system before replacing it

If treatment once worked, check:

  • bypass position;
  • pressure before and after the unit;
  • backwash frequency and drain flow;
  • valve programming;
  • injector or air-draw operation;
  • oxidant feed;
  • media fouling or age;
  • cartridge loading; and
  • water results before and after treatment.

A service fault may be repairable. Replacing equipment without confirming the cause can repeat the same failure.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my water come out clear and turn orange later?

That pattern is consistent with dissolved ferrous iron oxidizing after exposure to air, but a laboratory result is still needed before choosing treatment.

Are orange deposits always iron bacteria?

No. Dissolved iron, particulate iron and corrosion can also produce orange deposits. Slime, odour and repeated well fouling make iron bacteria more plausible but do not prove it.

Will a cartridge filter remove iron?

It can capture iron that is already particulate. Dissolved iron can pass through until it is oxidized or otherwise removed.

Can I use a softener for iron?

Sometimes, within the manufacturer's documented limits and when the iron is dissolved and the overall chemistry is suitable. It is not the right answer for every iron problem.

Should I shock-chlorinate the well myself?

Routine well disinfection after appropriate work can be done using official instructions, but recurring iron bacteria or well rehabilitation can require high concentrations and physical cleaning. Use a qualified well professional and never mix chlorine with acids.
