Water Testing in Victoria, BC: What Should You Test Before Buying Water Treatment?

A useful water test answers a specific question. Start with the water source and symptom, then choose the laboratory or field tests that can actually change the treatment decision.

Published October 5, 2026 · Pure Water Pros

If you are considering a water filter, softener, reverse-osmosis system, UV unit or private-well treatment system in Victoria, Langford or elsewhere on Vancouver Island, the most useful first question is not “Which system should I buy?” It is “What exactly is in the water, and what problem am I trying to solve?”

Water testing does not need to mean ordering every parameter on a laboratory menu. The right test depends on whether the home is supplied by Greater Victoria municipal water or a private well, what symptoms are present, and whether the concern is health-related, aesthetic or operational.

Pure Water Pros is not a laboratory and does not replace public-health guidance. Our role is to help homeowners understand which information is useful for a treatment decision, interpret ordinary treatment-related results, and match equipment to verified conditions rather than assumptions.

Municipal water and private-well water should not be approached the same way

The Capital Regional District monitors Greater Victoria municipal drinking water extensively. The CRD says the regional supply is tested routinely and meets applicable provincial requirements, with approximately 35,000 laboratory tests conducted each year across the system.

For many homeowners on municipal water, that means you do not need a broad private laboratory panel before every filtration decision. If the concern is a known aesthetic issue such as disinfectant taste or odour, the CRD’s published data may already answer many of the source-water questions.

A private well is different. The homeowner is responsible for the water source, and well quality can vary from one property to the next. HealthLink BC recommends regular bacteriological and chemical testing of private wells, and Island Health directs private-well owners to independent laboratories for analysis.

When should a municipal-water homeowner consider testing?

Even when the public supply is monitored, a homeowner may still have a reason to test water at the tap. Examples include:

  • An unusual taste, odour, colour or sediment condition that is isolated to the property.
  • Concern about plumbing or fixtures rather than the municipal source.
  • A treatment system that needs to be checked for performance.
  • A specific contaminant question that is not answered by the available CRD reporting.
  • A home renovation, plumbing change or long period of vacancy followed by a water-quality concern.

The CRD notes that its own laboratory is not set up to accept samples from the public. Homeowners who want their own tap water analyzed can use a qualified commercial drinking-water laboratory.

Greater Victoria municipal water is already very soft

This matters because hardness is one of the most common reasons homeowners are sold equipment they may not need. The CRD reports Greater Victoria drinking water as very soft, with a median hardness of about 17.4 mg/L as calcium carbonate, roughly one grain per gallon.

That means a homeowner on the regional municipal supply should not assume that white residue, spots or another household symptom automatically means a whole-home softener is required. Before buying one, confirm the water source and test hardness if the symptom truly points in that direction.

What should a private well be tested for?

HealthLink BC separates private-well testing into two broad categories: bacteriological testing and chemical testing. Bacteriological testing commonly includes total coliform and E. coli. Chemical testing can include parameters such as nitrate, metals and other constituents selected around the well and local conditions.

For treatment design, additional operational parameters may matter even when they are not the main health question. Depending on the complaint and the equipment being considered, useful information can include:

  • Hardness
  • Iron and manganese
  • pH and alkalinity
  • Turbidity and sediment
  • Total dissolved solids
  • Sulfur-related conditions where odour is present
  • UV transmittance where UV design requires it
  • Nitrate or other chemistry identified by the laboratory or health authority

The list should be driven by the source and the problem. A rotten-egg odour, orange staining, low pH, sediment, hardness scale and a failed bacteriological test are different problems and should not be treated as interchangeable.

If the concern is bacteria, use a qualified laboratory

Home test strips and handheld meters are not substitutes for proper bacteriological testing. If a private-well owner needs to know whether E. coli or total coliform bacteria are present, the sample should be handled and analyzed according to laboratory instructions.

HealthLink BC recommends bacteriological testing of private wells at least annually and after changes in clarity, colour or taste. It also notes that well-water quality can change after events such as flooding, drought or other changes in source conditions.

If a laboratory reports a health-related exceedance, follow the laboratory and health-authority guidance. Treatment equipment should not be used as a reason to ignore a failed test or a source-water problem.

What can field testing tell you?

Field testing is useful when the question is operational rather than regulatory. A hardness kit can help determine whether a softener is appropriate or whether an existing softener is working. A chlorine test can help assess disinfectant reduction through a carbon system. A conductivity or TDS meter can be useful when comparing RO feed water with product water.

Those measurements can be valuable for service work, but they answer narrower questions than a laboratory analysis. A TDS number does not tell you which dissolved substances are present. A hardness result does not tell you whether bacteria are present. A chlorine reading does not describe metals, nitrate or every other possible constituent.

What should you test for before buying a water softener?

Start with hardness. If the home is on Greater Victoria municipal water, the regional data already indicate very soft source water, so a homeowner should have a specific reason before adding softening equipment.

For a private well, test the actual well. If iron, manganese, sediment or sulfur are also present, those conditions may change the treatment design and may need to be addressed before or alongside softening.

What should you test for before buying an iron or sulfur system?

Do not select iron or sulfur equipment from odour or staining alone. Iron can exist in different forms, manganese may be involved, sulfur odour can come from more than one source, and pH affects treatment performance.

For a private well with staining or rotten-egg odour, the useful starting point is a water analysis combined with a description of where and when the symptom appears. Hot-water-only odour may point toward the water heater; hot-and-cold odour may justify investigating the well and source-water chemistry.

What should you test for before installing UV?

UV treatment is a disinfection step, not a general water filter. If UV is being considered for a private well, bacteriological testing is important, but treatment design also depends on whether the water is clear enough for UV light to work effectively.

Turbidity, iron, manganese, hardness and other conditions can foul a quartz sleeve or interfere with treatment. Some UV designs also use UV-transmittance information. The complete system should be selected around water quality, peak flow and the manufacturer’s validated operating conditions.

Read more in our guide: Does your private well need UV treatment?

What should you test for before buying reverse osmosis?

For an RO system, the key question is what you want the system to reduce. Municipal-water homeowners may want a dedicated drinking-water treatment step for taste, dissolved constituents or a specific certified reduction claim. Private-well owners may need more pretreatment depending on hardness, iron, sediment, pressure and other conditions.

A general TDS reading can help measure membrane performance, but it does not prove reduction of every contaminant. If a specific contaminant is the reason for buying RO, verify that the selected system has the appropriate certification or performance claim for that contaminant.

Do not buy treatment from a single number

A common mistake is taking one result—TDS, hardness, iron, pH or another value—and treating it as the entire water problem. Water chemistry interacts with equipment design. Flow rate, pressure, plumbing size, household demand, drain access, maintenance expectations and existing equipment all matter.

A good treatment recommendation answers several questions together:

  • What is the water source?
  • What symptom or test result is driving the concern?
  • Which result actually needs treatment?
  • What peak flow must the system handle?
  • What pretreatment does the equipment require?
  • How will performance be verified after installation?
  • What maintenance will be required?

Water testing in Victoria, Langford and the West Shore: a practical path

If you are on municipal water in Victoria, Langford, Colwood, View Royal or another CRD-served community, start with the CRD’s published water-quality information and the exact problem you are experiencing. If you need property-specific laboratory data, use a qualified commercial drinking-water laboratory.

If you are on a private well in Metchosin, Sooke, Highlands, the Saanich Peninsula or elsewhere on Vancouver Island, regular laboratory testing is part of responsible well ownership. Use the results to guide treatment instead of choosing equipment first and hoping it matches the water.

Pure Water Pros can review ordinary water-test results alongside the symptoms, water source, existing treatment equipment and household requirements. Where a public-health or laboratory interpretation is required, we will point you back to the appropriate laboratory or health authority rather than pretending a field service visit replaces them.

Frequently asked questions

Where can I get my tap water tested in Victoria, BC?

The CRD monitors the regional municipal supply, but its laboratory is not set up for public submissions. Homeowners who want their own tap water analyzed can use a qualified commercial drinking-water laboratory and follow that laboratory’s sampling instructions.

What should a private well be tested for?

Private wells should receive regular bacteriological and chemical testing. The exact chemical panel should be selected around the well, local conditions, previous results, symptoms and treatment goals.

Can a home test strip replace a laboratory test?

No for health-related or documented laboratory questions. Field tests are useful for operational screening such as hardness, chlorine or treatment performance, but they are not equivalent to a qualified laboratory analysis.

Should I buy treatment equipment before testing?

Usually not. The most defensible approach is to identify the water source and problem, obtain the relevant test information, and then select equipment around the result, required flow and maintenance conditions.

Test first. Treat second.
