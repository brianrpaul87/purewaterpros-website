Light-Commercial Water Treatment in Greater Victoria: Where Businesses Actually Need Filtration (and Where They Don’t)

For cafés, shops and smaller workplaces, the right treatment depends on the water source, the equipment and the actual concern—not a one-size-fits-all package.

Published October 21, 2026 · Brian Paul, Pure Water Pros

If the coffee at your café tastes different from one day to the next, a beverage machine is clogging, or you are considering a filter for your shop, the first step is to identify what the equipment or process actually needs.

Greater Victoria’s municipal water is generally very soft and is safe to drink. Most light-commercial premises connected to the municipal supply do not need a water softener or a whole-building treatment package just because they are a business. Filtration may still make sense at a specific tap or piece of equipment: for a defined taste, sediment or process concern.

Start with the job the water needs to do

A café, salon, clinic or small office may use water in several different ways: drinking, making beverages, rinsing, humidification or feeding a particular machine. Each use can have different requirements.

Start by noting where the issue occurs and what has changed. Does it affect the whole premises or only one machine? Is it a taste concern, visible particles, a drop in flow, scale inside equipment, or a manufacturer’s stated water requirement? These details help distinguish water quality from plumbing, maintenance or equipment issues.

The CRD’s 2025 Greater Victoria Drinking Water Quality Annual Report says the region’s drinking water was safe to drink throughout 2025. The CRD drinking-water FAQ lists a regional median hardness of 17.4 mg/L as calcium carbonate, supporting the practical point that the municipal supply is very soft.

That local context matters. A water softener intended for hard-water scale is not a default commercial upgrade in Victoria or Langford. If you are seeing scale, check the affected equipment and water source before assuming the whole building needs softening.

Common business concerns, and what may help

Taste or odour in drinks

A taste preference may make filtration worthwhile at a beverage station, even when the municipal supply meets drinking-water requirements. The aim in that case is a specific change in taste, not to suggest that the incoming water is unsafe.

Greater Victoria uses chloramine as part of its municipal disinfection process. If a business wants to reduce a chloramine-related taste or odour, ordinary activated carbon should not be assumed to provide the same reduction as a correctly selected catalytic-carbon product or a filter with a substantiated chloramine-reduction claim. Product performance depends on design, water conditions, flow and capacity.

For a café, the choice also depends on how much water the equipment draws during busy periods. A cartridge that works at a low draw may not suit a machine with higher or repeated demand. Check the specific reduction claim, rated capacity and flow requirements rather than choosing by a broad “carbon filter” label.

Sediment or particles

A sediment filter may be useful when particles are observed at a particular location or when an equipment manufacturer specifies particulate protection. But a filter is not a general fix for every cloudy-water complaint, clog or pressure problem.

First check whether particles are limited to one tap or machine, whether the issue is new, and whether the aerator, inlet screen or equipment filter needs service. Persistent discolouration or a sudden change throughout the premises should also be reported to the water supplier or property manager; a point-of-entry filter may not be the right first response.

Equipment protection

Some commercial equipment has its own water-quality requirements. Espresso machines, ice makers, humidifiers and other appliances may specify limits or treatment needs in their installation or service documents. Those requirements should guide the assessment.

A treatment stage can affect flow and pressure as well as water composition. A system that reduces one unwanted characteristic may not meet a different machine’s specification. Before choosing equipment, compare the manufacturer’s stated requirements with the actual water and the site’s peak demand.

Reverse osmosis for a specific use

Reverse osmosis (RO) can be appropriate when a particular drinking-water or process application calls for water with fewer dissolved minerals. It is not automatically the best choice for every café or office. RO needs suitable feed conditions, space, drainage and a plan for the water the system produces and rejects. It also has storage and production limits that should be matched to actual demand.

If you are considering RO for a beverage machine, confirm the equipment maker’s requirements first. The right answer for one appliance may not be right for every tap or use in the building.

A practical assessment, before selecting equipment

An assessment should clarify the concern and site conditions before anyone recommends a system. The scope and next step depend on the request; the following questions help organize the discussion.

  1. Describe the symptom. Note where it occurs, when it began and whether it affects every outlet or only a particular use.
  2. Identify the source and equipment. Confirm whether the premises use municipal water or another source. Share relevant equipment models, manuals and any treatment already installed.
  3. Check the site and demand. Review plumbing access, available space, flow and pressure, and how much water the process needs at peak use.
  4. Compare suitable options. Where appropriate, compare targeted filtration, sediment protection, RO or no additional treatment. Each option should include what it is intended to address, what it will not solve, and its maintenance needs.
  5. Verify and maintain. After installation, check relevant performance measures and explain how to operate and maintain the equipment. Cartridge or media service needs vary with water quality, use, system design and manufacturer instructions; there is no single replacement interval that fits every site.

Keep filtration separate from backflow requirements

Water filtration and cross-connection control are different concerns. Commercial properties may have backflow-prevention requirements based on their plumbing and processes. The CRD provides information on cross-connection control and backflow assemblies. Confirm what applies to your premises with the CRD and an appropriately qualified plumbing or backflow professional.

Frequently asked questions

Does a small business in Greater Victoria need a water softener?

Usually not just because it is a business. CRD reports very soft municipal water in the region. If you are seeing scale, check the specific equipment, water source and site conditions before considering a softener.

Is filtration required to make Greater Victoria municipal water safe?

No. CRD reports that Greater Victoria’s municipal drinking water is safe to drink. Filtration may be chosen for a taste preference, equipment specification or a defined operational need, not as a general safety requirement.

Can any carbon filter reduce chloramine taste?

No. Ordinary activated carbon is not automatically equivalent to catalytic carbon or a product with a substantiated chloramine-reduction claim. Check product-specific performance, capacity and flow suitability.

Should a café use RO for its espresso machine?

Only if the machine’s requirements and site conditions support it. Review the manufacturer’s water specification, demand, available space, drainage and maintenance needs before deciding.
