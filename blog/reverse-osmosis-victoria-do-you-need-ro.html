Do You Need Reverse Osmosis in Victoria, BC?

What RO actually does, what Greater Victoria's low-hardness municipal water means, and when a drinking-water system is worth installing.

Published September 11, 2026 · Pure Water Pros

If your home is supplied by Greater Victoria municipal water, the short answer is: you probably do not need reverse osmosis for basic water safety. You may still want it for a specific drinking-water goal, taste preference or contaminant-reduction objective.

That distinction matters. Reverse osmosis is excellent technology, but it is not automatically the right answer simply because someone wants “better water.” Start with the water source and the result you actually want.

Greater Victoria water starts from a very different place than hard-water regions

The Capital Regional District monitors and treats the regional municipal supply. In its 2024 annual report, the CRD listed median hardness of 16.9 mg/L as CaCO3 in the regional transmission mains and 17.2 mg/L in the Westshore distribution system. Those are low hardness values.

That means the usual sales pitch built around severe scale and very hard municipal water does not fit Victoria, Langford or Colwood particularly well. If your concern is chlorine or chloramine taste, a softener is not the treatment for that problem either.

For municipal-water homes, the better first question is usually: what do you want to change about the water at the kitchen tap?

What does reverse osmosis actually do?

An RO system uses water pressure to move water across a semipermeable membrane. Some water passes through the membrane as treated water, while a separate concentrate stream carries rejected dissolved material to drain.

Residential RO systems are usually point-of-use systems installed under a kitchen sink. A typical setup includes sediment and carbon prefiltration, the membrane itself, a storage tank or direct-flow pump arrangement, and a final polishing stage depending on the design.

RO membranes are commonly spiral-wound: membrane leaves wrap around a central permeate tube while feed water travels across the element.

Do all RO systems remove the same contaminants?

No. This is one of the biggest points missed in RO marketing.

NSF/ANSI 58 covers point-of-use reverse-osmosis drinking-water systems. The standard includes material safety, structural integrity, TDS reduction, efficiency and recovery information, plus verified contaminant-reduction claims.

TDS reduction is required under the standard. Other reduction claims can include substances such as lead, nitrate/nitrite, arsenic, fluoride, copper and certain VOCs, but those claims need to be verified for the particular certified system.

So “it has an RO membrane” is not enough information. If you are buying a system for a specific concern, look for the certification and performance claim for that concern.

Why does the carbon prefilter matter?

The membrane gets most of the attention, but the pretreatment stages matter too. Carbon can improve taste and odour and is commonly used ahead of an RO membrane. Depending on the system and cartridge, it may also reduce oxidants that could affect membrane life.

Greater Victoria uses chloramine as a distribution disinfectant, so the words “carbon filter” alone do not tell you how well a cartridge will perform. Cartridge type, verified claims, flow, capacity and replacement interval all matter. We cover that in more detail in our Greater Victoria chloramine guide.

A carbon block forces water through porous carbon before it reaches the centre core. The actual reduction claims depend on the specific certified cartridge.

When does RO make sense in Victoria or Langford?

For a home on CRD municipal water, I would consider RO when the homeowner has a clear drinking-water objective, such as:

  • wanting a dedicated source of low-TDS drinking water;
  • wanting treated water for drinking, cooking, coffee, tea or ice;
  • having a specific reduction goal supported by an appropriately certified system;
  • preferring RO water after comparing it with a simpler carbon drinking-water filter; or
  • wanting a point-of-use system rather than treating every litre entering the house.

If the only complaint is taste or odour, a good carbon filter may be simpler and less expensive. RO is not automatically “better”; it is a different treatment method.

What about minerals and remineralization?

RO reduces dissolved minerals including calcium and magnesium. That often leads to claims that RO water must be remineralized for health.

Health Canada states that drinking water is a minor source of these minerals compared with food, and that someone eating a reasonably balanced diet does not need a mineral supplement simply because they drink RO-treated water.

Some people still prefer the taste of remineralized water, and some systems add a remineralization cartridge for that reason. That is a preference and system-design choice, not something every homeowner automatically needs.

Does reverse osmosis waste water?

RO does create a concentrate stream that goes to drain. But a blanket statement such as “every RO wastes three or four gallons for every gallon produced” is too simplistic.

Efficiency depends on membrane design, feed pressure, temperature, recovery configuration and the specific system. NSF/ANSI 58 includes efficiency and recovery ratings, so a better comparison uses the actual certified product data rather than a generic number copied from the internet.

What changes on a private well?

A private well is a different design problem. Iron, manganese, hardness, sediment, sulfur odour, bacteria and other source-water conditions can affect whether RO is appropriate and what pretreatment is required.

RO should not be used as a substitute for understanding the well. If the raw water has a whole-home treatment problem, deal with that problem first and then decide whether a kitchen RO makes sense as the final drinking-water stage.

What should be checked before an RO installation?

Before selecting a system, I would want to know:

  • municipal water or private well;
  • incoming pressure and any pressure-regulating equipment;
  • available cabinet space and drain access;
  • whether there is existing treatment upstream;
  • the actual drinking-water goal;
  • the certified reduction claims of the proposed system; and
  • filter, membrane and sanitizing maintenance requirements.

That is much more useful than shopping by the number of “stages” printed on the box.
