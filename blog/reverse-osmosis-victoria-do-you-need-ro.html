Do You Need Reverse Osmosis in Victoria, BC?

If you're considering an under-sink RO, here's what it actually does, what our very soft municipal water changes, and when the extra treatment is worth having.

Published September 11, 2026 · Pure Water Pros

If your home is on Greater Victoria municipal water, you do not need reverse osmosis simply to make the water safe to drink. The CRD already treats and monitors the regional supply. That said, plenty of homeowners still like having RO at the kitchen sink because they prefer the taste, want lower dissolved solids, or have a specific reduction goal in mind.

That is really the useful way to think about RO here. It can be a very good drinking-water system, but whether it is worth installing depends on what you want to change about the water — not on the idea that every home should have one.

First, a useful Victoria-specific fact: our municipal water is very soft

The Capital Regional District's 2024 annual report lists median hardness of 16.9 mg/L as CaCO3 in the regional transmission mains and 17.2 mg/L in the Westshore distribution system. In practical terms, that is very soft water.

Those numbers matter because a lot of generic water-treatment advice online assumes the homeowner is dealing with hard water and scale. Around Victoria, Langford and Colwood, that assumption can lead to equipment being recommended for a problem that is not really there.

If what bothers you is the taste or smell of chlorine or chloramine at the kitchen tap, that is a different issue again. A softener is not designed to fix it, and RO may or may not be necessary depending on how much treatment you actually want.

What an RO system actually does

Reverse osmosis uses household water pressure to push water across a semipermeable membrane. Part of the water passes through the membrane as treated water, while another stream carries the rejected dissolved material to the drain.

Most residential systems are installed under the kitchen sink and include sediment and carbon prefiltration ahead of the membrane. From there, the treated water is either stored in a small pressure tank or delivered through a direct-flow design, depending on the system.

A typical residential RO membrane is spiral-wound. Feed water travels across the element, treated water moves toward the centre tube, and the concentrate stream exits separately.

One thing worth knowing before comparing systems is that two RO units can look almost identical and still have different verified performance. NSF/ANSI 58 covers point-of-use reverse-osmosis systems and includes requirements for things such as material safety, structural integrity and TDS reduction. Specific contaminant-reduction claims — for example lead, nitrate/nitrite, arsenic or fluoride — need to be verified for the particular certified system.

So if you are buying RO because of one specific concern, the certification and performance sheet matter more than the number of stages printed on the box.

Do not overlook the carbon filter ahead of the membrane

The RO membrane gets most of the attention, but the carbon stage does important work too. It can improve taste and odour, and depending on the cartridge and system design, it can help protect the membrane from oxidants in the incoming water.

That is especially relevant in Greater Victoria because the municipal system uses chloramine as a distribution disinfectant. A cartridge labelled simply “carbon” does not tell you everything you need to know. Media type, flow, capacity, replacement interval and verified performance all matter. We go into that in more detail in our Greater Victoria chloramine guide.

A carbon-block cartridge sends water through porous carbon before it reaches the centre core. What it is actually certified to reduce depends on the specific cartridge.

So when is RO worth installing in Victoria or Langford?

For most homes on CRD municipal water, RO makes the most sense when the goal is clearly about drinking water rather than whole-home treatment. Common reasons include:

  • wanting a dedicated source of low-TDS water at the kitchen sink;
  • wanting treated water for drinking, cooking, coffee, tea or ice;
  • having a specific reduction goal that is supported by an appropriately certified system; or
  • simply preferring the taste of RO water after comparing it with a good carbon filter.

If someone is mainly unhappy with disinfectant taste or odour, a quality carbon drinking-water filter may be all they need. If they want broader dissolved-solids reduction or a certified claim for a particular substance, RO becomes a much more sensible conversation.

Two questions that come up almost every time

Does RO strip out useful minerals?

It does reduce dissolved minerals, including calcium and magnesium. That sometimes gets turned into a sales argument that RO water must be remineralized before it is healthy to drink.

Health Canada takes a much less dramatic view: drinking water is a minor source of these minerals compared with food, and someone eating a reasonably balanced diet does not need a mineral supplement just because they drink RO-treated water.

Some people do prefer the taste of remineralized water, so a remineralization stage can still be a perfectly reasonable option. It is just not something every household automatically needs.

How much water goes to drain?

Every conventional RO system creates a concentrate stream, so some water does go to drain. The amount varies quite a bit between systems, though, which is why the old rule that “RO wastes three or four gallons for every gallon it makes” is not a very useful way to shop.

Membrane design, feed pressure, water temperature and the system's recovery configuration all affect efficiency. NSF/ANSI 58 includes efficiency and recovery information, so actual product data is a better comparison than a generic ratio.

Private wells need a different conversation

On a private well, we would not jump straight to the kitchen RO without first understanding the raw water. Iron, manganese, hardness, sediment, sulfur odour, bacteria and other conditions can all affect the system and may require treatment before the water ever reaches an RO membrane.

In that situation, RO is often the finishing stage for drinking water rather than the solution to the whole well. If the house has a broader water problem, it makes more sense to deal with that first and then decide what belongs under the kitchen sink.

What we would check before choosing a system

Before recommending an RO, we would want a quick picture of the water and the installation rather than choosing a unit from a catalogue first. That normally means checking:

  • whether the home is on municipal water or a private well;
  • incoming pressure and any pressure-regulating equipment;
  • cabinet space, drain access and the faucet location;
  • any treatment equipment already installed upstream;
  • what the homeowner actually wants the drinking water to be different from today; and
  • the certified claims and maintenance requirements of the proposed RO system.

That usually gets you to a better answer than comparing systems by stage count or assuming the most expensive unit must be the best one.
