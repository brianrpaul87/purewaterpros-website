Water Treatment System Maintenance in Victoria, BC: What May Be Due and What to Watch For

A filter may be overdue, an RO may be slowing down, or a UV lamp may still look lit after useful output has declined. Start with what changed before replacing equipment.

Published September 24, 2026 · Brian Paul, Pure Water Pros

Your water-treatment system may still be in place, but that does not always mean it is still performing as intended. A filter may be overdue. An RO system may be producing water more slowly. A UV lamp may still appear lit even though its useful output has declined. A softener may be using salt without properly regenerating. Equipment inherited with a home purchase may work well but have no clear maintenance record.

The useful question is not always, “How old is the system?” It is: what may be due, what has changed, and what should be checked before anything is replaced?

Maintenance needs depend on water quality, household usage, equipment design, operating conditions and the manufacturer's specifications. A typical interval can be a useful starting point, but it is not a guarantee.

Start with the symptom, not the replacement part

Before ordering a filter or replacing a complete system, note what changed and where you notice it.

  • Reduced flow from a drinking-water faucet
  • Pressure loss at several fixtures
  • Changes in taste, odour, colour or clarity
  • Hard water returning after a period of normal performance
  • A rotten-egg or other water odour returning on a private well
  • An RO storage tank that does not refill normally
  • A system running constantly or failing to regenerate
  • Water flowing continuously to a drain
  • Leaks, dampness or salt residue around equipment
  • An alarm, unusual noise or error message

A symptom may come from a filter, setting, valve, leak, pretreatment problem, pressure issue or change in the source water. Replacing one component without checking the rest of the system may not correct the cause.

What may be due on cartridge filters?

Sediment and cartridge filters collect material from the water. They can clog sooner when the source has a higher sediment load, when well conditions change, or when a filter is undersized for household flow.

Reduced flow or pressure loss can indicate that a cartridge is loaded. It can also indicate a closed valve, fouled treatment media, a plumbing restriction or a problem elsewhere in the system.

When considering water filter replacement in Victoria, BC, check what the filter is intended to remove, how much material it has been handling, whether flow changed gradually or suddenly, the cartridge's rated capacity and manufacturer instructions, and whether the housing, seals and shutoff valves are in good condition.

Cartridge life depends on the water passing through it, not only the number of months since installation. The manufacturer's rating and the actual condition of the filter should guide the decision.

What may be due on a reverse osmosis system?

An RO system normally has several service items: sediment and carbon prefilters, the RO membrane, a postfilter, tubing and fittings, the storage tank, faucet and drain connection, plus sanitizing where appropriate.

The prefilters protect the membrane and help control taste and odour. If they are exhausted or clogged, the RO system may produce water slowly and the membrane may be exposed to conditions it was not designed to handle.

Changes in RO water taste, reduced production, unusual storage-tank behaviour or higher dissolved-solids readings can point to different causes. A membrane lasts longer than most cartridge filters, but it is not permanent. Its useful life depends on feed-water quality, pretreatment, pressure, household demand and system design.

Membrane replacement should be based on performance testing and the manufacturer's specifications rather than a calendar date alone. A slow RO faucet may need a filter change, tank-pressure check, flow restriction check or membrane assessment, not necessarily a complete replacement.

What may be due on a UV system?

A UV system requires more than a lamp that appears to be switched on. On many residential systems, replacing the lamp about annually is typical according to the manufacturer's instructions. Some models have different lamp designs or service intervals, so the exact model matters. A lamp can remain visibly lit while its useful UV output has declined.

The quartz sleeve also needs inspection and cleaning. Mineral, iron or other deposits can reduce the amount of UV energy reaching the water. Cleaning frequency may need to increase when the source water causes faster fouling.

  • Lamp age and model
  • Quartz sleeve condition and cleanliness
  • Seals and fittings
  • Sediment and other pretreatment
  • Flow rate and system sizing
  • Alarm or dose-monitoring function
  • Bypass and shutoff arrangements

What may be due on a softener?

Softener maintenance is not limited to adding salt. Check whether the salt level is appropriate, whether a salt bridge has formed, whether salt has become wet or compacted, whether the unit is drawing brine during regeneration, whether the injector and brine line are clear, whether the settings are suitable, and whether treated water is still testing soft.

A softener that stops producing soft water may have a salt or brine problem, incorrect settings, a valve issue or fouled resin. Resin can lose capacity over time, particularly when exposed to conditions that foul it, but it does not automatically need routine replacement at a fixed age.

Test the treated water before assuming the resin is finished. If salt, regeneration and valve operation are correct but hardness continues to pass through, resin condition and equipment design can then be assessed.

What about carbon and other treatment media?

Activated carbon and catalytic carbon have a finite capacity. Once the media is exhausted, taste, odour or chlorine-related concerns may return. Higher demand, stronger disinfectant exposure and chloramine treatment requirements can shorten carbon life.

Ordinary activated carbon should not be assumed to provide the same chloramine reduction as appropriately selected catalytic carbon or certified chloramine-reduction equipment.

Tank-style systems may also contain media for iron, sulfur or oxidation. These systems can require backwashing, control-valve checks and eventual media replacement. The right service interval depends on the water test, media type, loading, flow and manufacturer's instructions.

Media should be assessed when symptoms return, test results change or the system no longer completes its cycle correctly. Time alone is not enough to determine whether replacement is needed.

When should equipment be inspected instead of replaced?

Inspection is usually the sensible next step when the equipment is old but otherwise sound, the problem started after a power interruption or setting change, the system has not been serviced but has no obvious damage, water quality changed gradually, a service item may be due but the rest of the system is unknown, the equipment was inherited with the home, or the same symptom returns soon after a component is replaced.

What to have ready when requesting service

You do not need to diagnose anything before asking for help. A few details make the first conversation more useful:

  • Whether the home is on municipal water or a private well
  • What equipment is installed, and any model or manufacturer details you can find
  • What has changed, and when you first noticed it
  • Whether the issue affects one fixture or the whole home
  • A photo of the system and mechanical room, if that is easy to take

If a microbiological concern is involved, that testing should be completed through an accredited laboratory or the appropriate public-health process.

Frequently asked questions

How often should water-treatment filters be replaced?

There is no single interval that applies to every home. Filter life depends on water quality, sediment load, household usage, cartridge design and the manufacturer's rating. Reduced flow or returning taste and odour may indicate that a filter should be checked sooner.

How often should an RO membrane be replaced?

RO membranes generally last longer than prefilters and postfilters, but they are not permanent. Feed-water quality, pretreatment, pressure and usage affect membrane life. Performance testing and the manufacturer's specifications should guide replacement.

Does a UV lamp need to be replaced if it is still lit?

Usually, yes, when the manufacturer's service interval has been reached. A lamp can remain visibly lit while its useful output has declined. The quartz sleeve, alarms, sensors and pretreatment should also be checked.

Can Pure Water Pros service equipment it did not install?

Pure Water Pros can service and assess existing equipment, including many systems installed by others, where appropriate. The system must be identifiable and accessible, and available service depends on the equipment, condition and site requirements.

Does a UV system confirm that well water is bacteriologically safe?

No. A UV system should be sized, installed and maintained appropriately, but that does not replace microbiological testing through an accredited laboratory or the appropriate public-health process.
